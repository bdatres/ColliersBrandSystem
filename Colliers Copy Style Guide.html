Colliers U.S.

Copy Style Guide.

Grammatical and style errors diminish our credibility and professionalism. Cleanly edited, accurate and succinctly compelling content helps readers immediately understand, engage and respond to our messages — while contributing to Colliers' brand success. Keep this guide handy as a grammar, punctuation and style reference for work in the U.S. For anything not covered here, refer to the current Associated Press (AP) Stylebook, or Colliers' global style guide for international communications.

Quick tips

The fifteen rules people get wrong most.

Colliers’ Possessive Form

Colliers’

Collier’s

Advisor vs. Adviser

advisor

adviser

Square Feet Abbreviation

sq. ft. (body copy) · SF (charts, ads, bullets, incomplete sentences)

sq. ft or sq ft

Job Title Capitalization

Jane Smith, managing director · Vice President Stan Jones

Capitalize only when the title immediately precedes a name.

Business Lines & Team Names

Roberts Debt Advisory Group · Klein Valuation Team

Always capitalized.

Services & Sectors

…retail, office and industrial sectors.

Not capitalized, unless part of a team name.

British English Spellings

Use U.S. English in the U.S.

color / zip code — not color / postal code

Million and Billion

$500 million / $500B

Spell out in copy; abbreviate M/B in tables & headlines.

Numbers Usage

one / two / three / 10 / 11 / 12

One to nine spelled out, 10+ numerical.

ALL CAPS

Sentence case is readable.

Do not use title case or ALL CAPS, even in bulleted lists.

Dates and Numbers

June 21, 2019 · June 21 (no year)

Never use nth, rd, st, th in a date.

Company Names

Construction Company

Remove Plc./Ltd./LLC etc. from client lists.

Internet Terms Hyphenation

email / online / website

e-mail / on-line / web-site

And vs. Ampersand (&)

Engineering & Design

Use “&” only in service line names, not body copy.

Nationalities and Languages

He was German. He spoke Spanish.

Title case only.

Spelling

Follow AP style as the baseline.

U.S. content creators should refer to the current Associated Press (AP) Stylebook for anything not covered in this guide. The printed AP Stylebook updates every other year; the digital version updates throughout the year. Make sure your Microsoft Office dictionary is set to English (United States). Refer to the Global Colliers Guide for British English and metric examples.

Copy

Formatting for names, dates, titles & more.

TermRuleExamples
and, & (ampersand)Spell out “and” in body copy; use “&” only in team, department and company names.She handles advisory and transactions. / The Smith Landlords & Tenants team / Engineering & Design
academic accreditationsName, comma, degree. List only the highest degree per field; no periods in degrees; commas separate multiple designations.Jane Smith, MAI / William Thornton, PhD / George Jetson, CCIM, CRE
✗ John Smith, M.A.I.
addressesNo end-of-line commas; no comma between place and zip. No “#” before suite/unit. Spell out street descriptors in body copy; abbreviate (with periods) in charts/tables/headlines.865 S. Figueroa Street, Suite 3500, Los Angeles, CA 90017
all capsNever use all caps except acronyms/initialisms of organizations — spell out on first use, acronym in parentheses after.Society of Industrial and Office Realtors (SIOR); SIOR events…
abbreviation / initialism / acronymAbbreviation = shortened form, always with a period, not all caps. Initialism = first letters, read as letters, usually all caps. Acronym = initialism pronounced as a word.Bldg., dept. / CRE, CEO, ROI / HUD, CAM, REIT
datesNo superscripts (th, rd, st). Numerically: MM/DD/YYYY with forward slashes, no spaces. Eras: no apostrophe.Wednesday, June 14 / 06/12/2026 / 1960s
✗ June 14th; 1960’s
days of the weekCapitalize; abbreviate only in tables.Monday, Tuesday
✗ Sun., Mon., Tues. (outside tables)
directionsCapitalize when designating a region/proper name; lowercase for compass direction. Abbreviate in street addresses (no period unless end of sentence).North Vancouver, West Virginia / south on Yonge Street / 20 W 34th St.
e.g. and i.e.e.g. = “for example”; i.e. = “in other words” — not interchangeable. Lowercase, periods, followed by a comma.Colliers offers many practice groups (e.g., transportation, healthcare…). Colliers’s style is based on AP (i.e., AP) Stylebook.
✗ eg, ie
electronic “e-”Hyphenated, lowercase “e” (except sentence-start). “email” and “esports” are never hyphenated/capitalized.e-business, e-commerce / email me the report.
email addressesFirst-name/last-name capitalization is optional (aids readability); never capitalize “colliers.”john.doe@colliers.com
✗ Colliers@...
etc. vs. et al.“etc.” completes a list of things; “et al.” completes a list of people. Both lowercase, both take periods (incl. mid-sentence); “etc.” takes commas before/after mid-sentence.…a dishwasher, microwave, updated carpets, etc., and they are leasing… / John Smith et al. agreed.
headlinesSentence case — capitalize first word and proper nouns only. Never all caps or small caps.Colliers reports solid second quarter results
job titlesCapitalize a formal title only when it immediately precedes a name; lowercase after an article/pronoun.Colliers Vice President Sam Smith / I talked to my managing director earlier.
login, log inOne word as noun/adjective; two words as a verb.He provided the login number. / She will log in to her platform.
monthsCapitalize.September, October, November
seasonsNever capitalize unless part of a date.Take-up increased in the summer months. / Summer 2025

✗ 7pm, 7 : 15
trademarksUse ™/® on first reference to a proper name if required by the business entity.The LEED® Certification trademark is owned by the USGBC.
web addresses / websiteDon't capitalize “colliers” or use “www.”; “website” is one word.colliers.com

Punctuation

Commas, quotes, dashes and lists.

TermRuleExamples
apostrophe (plural)Make an acronym, abbreviation or year plural by adding “s” with no apostrophe — except single-letter abbreviations, which do take one for clarity.Four VIPs were at the event. / 1980s / The Oakland A’s / four A’s and two B’s.
apostrophe (possessive)Singular words: add ’s. Plural words: add s’.a day’s pay / two weeks’ vacation (or rephrase: a two-week vacation)
parenthesesFinal period goes outside the parentheses when the parenthetical ends a sentence; no space between characters.(always).
✗ ( always) / (always.)
bulleted listsSpace after the bullet. Periods (not semicolons) end each item. Parallel construction across items — same part of speech, verb tense, sentence type, voice. No “and”/commas/semicolons at the end of items. Introduce with a short phrase.Bulleted list items should: Start with the same part of speech… Use the same verb tense… Use a phrase, if desired.
colon, semicolonOne space after a colon; capitalize after only for a proper noun or a full sentence. Semicolons separate more than a comma but less than a period — one space after, sparingly used, never to introduce bullets, no cap unless proper noun.Remember this: People do not want to be bothered. / Smith, Jones & Johnson; Webster & Thurston; and Thompson, Woodbridge & Lymon.
commaOne space after. No comma before Inc./Corp. Use for longer introductory phrases; omit if it would split the sentence into two complete parts. No comma before “and”/“or” in a simple series; do use one before a concluding conjunction in a complex series.apples, pears, bananas / FirstService Corp. is our previous owner.
✗ FirstService, Corp. / EastGroup Properties Inc., is…
ellipsis ( … )Three periods, space before and after, none between periods. Never at the end of a sentence.It was a pleasure working with Colliers … we knew we were in safe hands.
em-dash, en dash, hyphenEm-dash (width of “M”) signals an abrupt change, spaces before/after. En dash (width of “N”) for numerical ranges in headlines/tables only, no spaces. Hyphen joins compound words; use with “well-” combos before a noun (not after); use in modifiers of 3+ words.We are collaborative — and always will be. / January 25–27 / a mixed-use center / well-known expert / well known (after noun)
forward slash, backslashUse “/” to separate list items or in place of “or.” Never use “/” to mean “to”; never use a backslash.cash and/or credit cards / 2019–20 financial year
✗ 2019/20; 2023\24
periodsOnly one space after a period.The property sold. It was a record deal.
quotation marksDouble quotes for quotes; single quotes for a quote within a quote. Period and comma always go inside. Dash/semicolon/question mark/exclamation point go inside when they apply to the quoted matter, outside otherwise.“Upon closing the deal, our client said, ‘Excellent job!’ and we couldn’t be more pleased,” said the broker.

Numbers

Figures, currency, time and measurements.

TermRuleExamples
basis points (BPS)Spell out in body copy; BPS (caps) OK for subsequent references, headlines, captions. 1 bps = 0.01%; 1% = 100 bps.…fees at 100 basis points (BPS)… Colliers’ experts have tracked BPS averages… / Deal Closes at Record BPS!
billion, millionSpell out lowercase in body copy, one space before the word, round to hundredths. Abbreviate M/B (capitalized, no space) in tables/charts/headlines.$1 billion, $1.25 million / $1B, $1M
✗ $1 bn, $1 m, $1 Million
commas in numbersComma for numbers greater than 999.1,100 / 23,671
capitalization rate / cap rateSpell out “capitalization rate” on first reference (lowercase); “cap rate” OK after. Always a numeral + percentage.We sold that building at a 10% cap rate.
✗ a 10 cap rate; an eight CAP
datesNo superscripts. Numerically: MM/DD/YY with slashes, no spaces. Month before day when spelled out. No apostrophe for an era.Wednesday, June 14 / July 17, 2025 / 6/12/23 / 1960s
✗ 1970’s
decimals, percentagesNumerals, not words. “Percent” or “%” both fine. Round to two decimal places max; use a leading zero below 1.Rates increased by 4%. / Vacancy decreased by 0.5% this year. / 9.825% cap rate
foot, feet, square feet“Square foot” (no hyphen) as noun; hyphenate as a modifier; “square feet” no hyphen. Abbreviate “sq. ft.” (periods) for shorter content; “SF”/“M SF” reserved for research reports, headlines, tables.$22 per square foot / a 23,000-square-foot building / 300,000 square feet / 3M SF
fractionsHyphenate simple fractions as words; convert complex fractions to decimals/percentages when possible; numerals for precise amounts over one.Three-quarters of tenants agree. / 1.25 / One and one-quarter
✗ 1-3/4
H1, H2No comma between the term and year; acceptable in research reports, avoid elsewhere.We saw a strong performance in H1 2020.
miles, per square mileStandard U.S. units of distance/area.The retail outlet is 3 miles from the town center.
numbers 1–9Spell out one to nine (except units); 10+ numerically. Numerals for ages and units of measure.This should be completed in three years. / The 5-year-old building sold. / 3 acres
Quarter / Q1Capitalize “Quarter” + numeral when defining the date; “Q” + numeral, no space, optional year after a single space. Lowercase “quarter over quarter” / “quarter on quarter,” never hyphenated.Quarter 2 / Q2 2027
✗ quarter 1, 1Q, 1st quarter
telephone numbersU.S. numbers use dashes (parentheses optional for area code). International numbers: spaces, no dashes/periods/parentheses; U.S. country code is +1.714-499-4133 / (949) 555-1212 / +1 818 556 7140
timea.m./p.m. lowercase with periods, space after number, colon with no spaces. Hyphen (not em dash) between start/end times in lists.7 p.m. / 7:15 a.m. / 11-2 (lists) / 11:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. (body)
Time Zones“S” for Standard, “D” for Daylight; no commas or parentheses.7 p.m. PDT / 6 a.m. CST / 6 a.m. CDT

Which word is correct?

Commonly confused words.

WordRuleExamples
a / anUse “a” before consonant sounds, “an” before vowel sounds.an hour, an egg, an honor, a union, a helicopter, an MBA
accept / exceptAccept = to take something offered/endure. Except = to exclude or take exception.He accepted their offer for the building. / All of the offices were empty except one.
affect / effectAffect (verb) = to influence. Effect (verb) = to cause; (noun) = result.This change affects our professionals. / It’s hard to effect change. / a positive effect on the market.
among / between / among“Among” for 3+ nouns; “between” for two. Never “among.”This property is among the most expensive in the U.S. / I am choosing between these two options.
assure / ensure / insureAssure = make confident of an outcome. Ensure = guarantee. Insure = insurance only.The broker assured his clients… / …ensures a smooth transaction. / The building is fully insured.
complement / complimentComplement = to complete/supplement. Compliment = praise.The roles complement each other. / The client complimented Lisa on her expertise.
has / have“Has” for singular nouns/pronouns and for companies (single entities); “I have” in first person.Colliers has created a research report.
its / it’s“Its” shows possession; “it’s” = it is.Colliers has launched its annual campaign. / It’s raining today.
that / which“That”/“which” for objects, “who” for people. Always “which” after a comma.We recommend digital display marketing, which reaches more buyers. / the method that won more bids. / the broker who closed the deal.
their / there / they’reTheir = belonging to them. There = location. They’re = they are.Their broker is excellent. / The building is over there. / They’re on the way.
theirs / there’sTheirs = possessive pronoun (“their’s” is not a word). There’s = there is.The building is theirs. / There’s a restaurant around the corner.
U.S. / US / America“U.S.” (with periods) preferred; the period doubles as sentence punctuation at the end.That Colliers professional leads the U.S. in sales.
your / you’reYour = belonging to you. You’re = you are.We have closed your deal. / You’re a wonderful client.
